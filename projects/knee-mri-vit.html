Knee Injury Detection from MRI using Vision Transformers

A multi-view Vision Transformer that reads knee MRI scans from three angles and flags ACL tears, meniscus tears and general abnormalities.

Context
Applied Machine Learning (EEEM068), MSc Data Science, University of Surrey
Data
Stanford MRNet, ~1,130 knee MRI exams
Model
Multi-view ViT-Small

Overview

The goal was to see how well a Vision Transformer, a model originally built for everyday photos, could learn to spot sports-related knee injuries in medical scans. I used the Stanford MRNet dataset of around 1,130 real knee MRI exams.

The problem

Knee MRIs are 3D volumes captured from three planes (sagittal, coronal and axial), and a single patient can have more than one injury at once. The data is also heavily imbalanced: most scans are abnormal, while ACL tears are relatively rare. A naive model can score high accuracy just by predicting "abnormal" every time, so the real challenge was building something that genuinely detects the less common injuries.

Results

Macro ROC-AUC ~0.84 across the three labels
Macro F1 ~0.74–0.79 across the three labels
From-scratch baseline AUC ~0.74 same architecture, single plane, no pretraining

Training the same architecture from scratch on a single plane reached a noticeably lower AUC, which gave clear evidence that pretraining is essential on a dataset this small.

My approach

Multi-label setup

Treated the task as three independent yes/no predictions with sigmoid outputs, since a knee can have an ACL tear and a meniscus tear together.

Turning 3D into 2D

Took three slices from each MRI plane and stacked them as RGB channels, so a pretrained image model could use them directly.

Multi-view late fusion

Each plane goes through a ViT-Small backbone, and the three CLS token embeddings are concatenated before a custom MLP classification head.

Transfer learning

Started from SiT-S self-supervised pretrained weights, then fine-tuned with progressive layer unfreezing and layer-wise learning rate decay so the new head learned faster than the pretrained layers.

Handling imbalance

A WeightedRandomSampler controls how often rare cases are seen, and Focal BCE loss with per-label weights controls how much they matter during training.

Augmentation

Random rotation (about ±20°), translation and scaling, plus MixUp for regularisation.

Evaluation

Sensitivity, specificity, F1, balanced accuracy and ROC-AUC per label, with confusion matrices, rather than relying on plain accuracy.

What I learned

  • Why accuracy can be misleading on imbalanced medical data, and how to choose metrics that reflect real clinical usefulness.
  • How sampling and loss weighting solve different parts of the imbalance problem.
  • Practical fine-tuning of transformers: freezing strategies, differential learning rates and avoiding overfitting on small datasets.
  • Iterating properly: the project went through four versions, moving from a single-plane multiclass model to a multi-view multi-label system.
  • Thinking about the ethics of AI in healthcare, including dataset bias and why a model like this should support clinicians rather than replace them.
